
As office space density continues to increase, emergency evacuation efficiency has become a key determining factor for personnel safety. Although research in the fields of crowd dynamics and evacuation simulation is quite extensive, most of them focuses on the simulation accuracy of pedestrian models and the impact analysis of isolated parameters. There is still a lack of research on architectural space layout and multi-factor coupling effects.

Therefore this study aims to systematically explore the impact of internal spatial geometric configuration parameters (such as table layout, corridor width, number of exits and door width, etc.) on the efficiency of evacuation of a office space. The research will focus on the following questions: 
- How does the layout of furniture affect evacuation efficiency and path formation; 
- What is the marginal effect of corridor width and exit parameters on evacuation dynamics and the regulatory role of congestion patterns; 
- How would the gate width before exit influence the formation of the path; 
- What is the relationship between the evacuation time distribution and the path distribution under different gate width configurations. 

Through the simulation of two scale office prototypes, it reveals how these internal layout parameters systematically regulate the evacuation process, in order to provide data-driven optimization suggestions for the safety design of office.
### 1.2.1 Small prototype

For the small prototype study, there are three sub-questions.

RQ1: How do different island desk layouts affect evacuation efficiency?

In the Small prototype, three island desk layouts (ABC) will be tested under single and double exit scenarios, with corridor widths ranging from 0.8 m to 2.4 m. The study will compare how each layout affects evacuation time and the shape of routes, focusing on how desk placement influences path formation and overall efficiency.

RQ2: What is the marginal effect of corridor width on evacuation efficiency?

This question examines the corridor width effect within the 0.8 m to 2.4 m range, across different desk layouts (ABC) and exit numbers (single/double). The goal is to quantify how changes in width influence total evacuation time and to identify any nonlinear or threshold effects, as well as potential optimal width ranges.

RQ3: How does the number of exits regulate evacuation paths and speed?

This question compares evacuation performance across various desk layouts (ABC) and corridor widths under single and double exits. It looks at how exit number shapes path formation and evacuation speed, revealing systematic differences in path development, congestion patterns, and efficiency.
### 1.2.2 Big prototype

For the big prototype study, two sub-questions will be tested.

RQ4: How does the width of the gate in front of the exit affect evacuation efficiency?

In the Big prototype, the focus is on gate widths in the range of 1.0 to 1.6 m. The aim is to identify threshold effects of gate width and their impact on early congestion and evacuation speed. The study will test different gate widths while keeping other conditions constant, evaluating total evacuation time, route patterns, and congestion distribution.

RQ5: Coupling effects of room and corridor door widths

This question looks at MultiRoom nested layouts and how different combinations of room door widths and corridor door widths influence route formation, congestion patterns, and evacuation times. The study will compare several door-width combinations to reveal the systemic impact of door-width coupling on evacuation efficiency.
